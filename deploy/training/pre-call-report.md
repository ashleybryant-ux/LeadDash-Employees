# Pre-Call Report Training (the One Leak Play, added by Dr. Ashley, Oct 4, 2026)

Each "## [category] Title" section becomes one Brain entry named "Company training: Title". The method is John Lawson's One Leak Play, set up here for LeadDash's prospects: behavioral health private practices.

## [mission_profile] Pre-call report: the method

Before a sales call or demo with a practice, the team can run a Pre-Call Report (also called a Cold Read). It is research from public information only: no calls to the practice, no forms submitted, no logins. It takes about 15 minutes of searching, roughly 6 to 10 searches.

The report finds the single biggest place the practice is losing money, puts a dollar estimate on it from the practice's own public facts, and names one fix. Find one leak, prove it with their own numbers, and stop. Do not turn it into an audit. An audit is a project, and projects get thought about. One leak is a problem, and problems get fixed.

What's needed to start: the practice name and its city and state. More helps: phone, website, specialty. If the practice can't be identified (a name too common to search, no location), ask for the city or a link. Never ask for anything that can be found by searching.

Rules for every report:
- Public information only. Calls placed to the practice: always 0.
- Never a client's name or anything about a client, even if it appears in a public review. Quote the pattern ("three reviews mention unanswered calls"), never the person.
- Numbers over adjectives. Show the gap, don't editorialize about its size.
- The finding should read like something the practice could argue with in its own favor, not a sales pitch dressed up as research.
- If the research comes back thin (good hours, online booking, fresh reviews, strong local visibility), say so plainly. Don't invent a leak. Say this one needs the actual call.

## [mission_profile] Pre-call report: the four passes for a practice

Run the passes in this order.

**01 / SITE.** Open the practice's website. Can a new client book or request an appointment without calling? Online booking, an intake request form, a "check your insurance" step? Or is the only path a phone number, or a contact form and a wait? Note anything that reads as neglect: two different addresses, dead links, an old copyright year, a clinician list that doesn't match the directory listings, no online path at all.

**02 / HOURS.** Compare when the practice can be reached with when people actually reach out. People look for a therapist in the evening, on weekends and in a crisis, not only 9 to 5. Pull listed hours from the Google listing or the site. The finding: the gap between when someone is ready to book and when anyone at the practice can answer.

**03 / REVIEWS.** Pull the Google review count, average rating and how recent the reviews are. A low average isn't the main thing to check. Check whether the review engine is alive: 15 reviews mostly five years old with no owner replies is a different practice from one with a steady recent trickle. Note if reviews mention calls not returned or long waits for a first appointment.

**04 / RIVALS.** Search "best therapist in [city]", "[specialty] counseling [city]" and the Psychology Today directory for the city. Does the practice show up? If not, who leads instead? Pick the single strongest competitor (most reviews, or top of the list) and pull the same facts for them: review count, hours, online booking, weekend or evening availability, whether they take the insurance the target takes.

For a practice that runs mostly through referrals or contracts (EAPs, schools, agencies), keep the same questions: where does a referral get stuck, when is the practice unreachable, what is the trust signal and how fresh is it, and who is winning the referrals this practice should win.

## [mission_profile] Pre-call report: the one leak, the math and the move

**The one leak.** After the four passes, find the single root cause the other findings are symptoms of. A practice that can't be reached evenings and weekends usually also has stale reviews and missing directory rankings, and those are downstream of the same gap. Name the one costing the most and fastest to prove, then mention (don't detail) that there are others.

The leaks that show up in almost every practice:
- Inquiries that go to voicemail or sit unanswered, so the person books with the next practice on the list.
- Intake that dies between the first call and the first session (no follow-up, paperwork that never gets sent).
- Reviews nobody has asked for in years.
- Claims and balances aging past when they should have been collected.
- Staff typing the same client into separate marketing and EHR systems.

Lead with whichever has the biggest number and the fastest proof for this practice.

**The math.** Build a short table from this practice's own findings (days closed, hours unreachable, review gap against the rival) times one sourced value: the practice's own listed session fee if public, otherwise a sourced typical self-pay session rate for that state, and a typical number of sessions per new client. Land the result as a monthly estimate labeled as a placeholder. The real number comes from the call, once the practice shares its actual call volume or inquiry count. Never present it as an industry statistic.

Example rows:

| Assumption | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Days per week no one answers new inquiries | n of 7 |
| Weekday evening hours unreachable | n |
| Estimated new-client inquiries missed per week | n |
| Session fee (their own, or sourced for the state) | $n |
| Sessions in a typical first month | n |
| Estimated revenue walking to the next practice, per month | about $n (placeholder) |

**The move.** Recommend exactly one fix, using something the practice already has (its phone number, its website, its client list), never a rebuild. For LeadDash this is usually one piece of the platform: inquiries answered and booked any hour on the number they already use, online booking on the site they already have, an automatic review request after a session, or one record from first call to paid claim so nobody types a client twice. Say what it does in plain words, then: "That's the entire first move." Everything else found gets addressed after, if at all.

## [voice_tone] Pre-call report: how it reads and how it's laid out

Voice: short sentences, lead with the point, direct and plain, a little dry. No em dashes. No hype words (leverage, synergy, revolutionary, game-changing). No "this isn't X, it's Y" lines.

Sections, in this order:

1. **Header.** PRE-CALL REPORT and the practice name, then: Practice, Founded (if findable), Phone, BBB status (if findable), Website, Address on file, Prepared by (the owner, LeadDash), Research time, and "Calls placed to the practice: 0".
2. **The read.** 2 to 4 sentences. One line naming the tension (an asset the practice has that doesn't show up where people look), then what's real about them and how little of it a new client sees before calling. Close: "Here's the fifteen-minute pass, in order."
3. **The walk.** The four passes, each with a one-line verdict as its heading (for example "Open weekdays 9 to 5. Closed the evenings people actually call.") and 1 or 2 short paragraphs.
4. **The numbers.** One line naming the comparison, two pulled-out stats side by side (target against the rival, like review counts), then a small table: days open, latest hour reachable, evening or weekend availability, online booking, listed in the city's "best of" or directory results.
5. **The one leak.** The finding in one paragraph, with everything else framed as a symptom of it.
6. **The math.** The assumptions table, then a closing note that the figure is a placeholder and the real number comes from the practice's own call log or inquiry count.
7. **The move.** The one fix, tied to something they already have.
8. **Next step.** Fifteen minutes of research is not a substitute for fifteen minutes on the phone. If the estimate is off, that's what the call is for. Then how to reach the owner, with the LeadDash booking link.
